Concrete Parking Lots in the Kansas City metro

The hardest-working pavement on a commercial property is a small fraction of it. A loaded trash truck stops at the dumpster pad on the same wheel lines every week. Delivery trucks turn hard on the dock aprons, and every car on site funnels through the same entrance lanes. Asphalt gives under loads like that, a little at a time, until the pad is rutted and the apron has shoved out of shape. Concrete doesn't give, and that difference is most of the argument for a concrete lot. Built on a properly compacted base, the areas that take the worst abuse become the ones you stop thinking about.

A full concrete lot isn't always the right answer. Much of our commercial work mixes the two materials on one site. We pour concrete where trucks stop and turn, then pave the stall areas in asphalt, because a parked car is about the lightest duty a pavement sees. Years later the asphalt sections can be overlaid without touching the concrete. Walking the site costs you nothing, and when two layouts are in play the proposal carries a separate price for each, so you're weighing real numbers instead of a hunch.

How a commercial slab ages is mostly decided on paper, before any concrete is ordered. We lay out sawcut joints from the slab thickness, keep panels near square, and avoid re-entrant corners at islands and drain inlets so the cracking follows the joints instead of wandering across panels. Reinforcement gets matched to traffic, with plain jointed panels where cars park and thicker or steel-reinforced sections under aprons and dumpster pads. ADA belongs in the design stage too, because the slope limits at accessible parking and along the path to the entrance are set by the grading plan, not the paint.

Common questions

Concrete Parking Lots FAQs

We can't close during construction. How does that work?

In stages. We keep a route open to your doors and receiving, pour one portion of the lot at a time, and don't move on until the finished section is back in service. Fresh panels can't carry a car for days after the pour, so the sequence gets built around cure windows rather than crew convenience. You get a phasing map before work starts showing which stalls close on which days, and we schedule the loudest, dustiest parts for whatever hours hurt you least.

Is a concrete lot worth the higher price compared to asphalt?

Sometimes, and it mostly depends on how long you plan to own the property. Figure 25 to 30 years or more for a concrete lot against 15 to 20 for asphalt. By the time an asphalt lot has been resurfaced and patched a few times, a concrete lot poured the same year is usually still on its original surface. Spread over decades of ownership, concrete's cost per year often comes out even or ahead. If you expect to sell within a few years, asphalt's lower first cost probably serves you better.

What maintenance does a concrete parking lot actually need?

Mostly joint care. The sawcuts get filled with a flexible sealant that keeps water and grit out of the joints. That sealant hardens and pulls away over time, and once water works under a panel through an open joint, freeze-thaw cycles and traffic start rocking and settling the slab. Have the joints checked every couple of years and plan on resealing every five to ten years depending on exposure. It is a small job next to replacing settled panels, which is where neglected joints end up.

Does concrete parking lot work stop for the winter?

The pours mostly do. Concrete needs unfrozen subgrade and a run of workable temperatures, and KC winters only offer those in stretches. Demolition, grading, and base work can keep going in colder weather than finishing can, which is why winter starts sometimes make sense for phased jobs. The tear-out and base repair get done through the cold months, and each section sits ready for concrete as soon as the thermometer cooperates.
