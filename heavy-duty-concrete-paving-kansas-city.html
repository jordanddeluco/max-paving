Heavy-Duty Concrete Truck Lanes & Warehouse Paving in the Kansas City metro

A loaded semi concentrates its weight onto a few small contact patches, and the worst damage happens at low speed, while the truck is turning, backing into a dock, or braking in the same spots every shift. Under that kind of loading asphalt deforms. It ruts where wheels dwell, shoves into ridges at the dock doors, and breaks up under parked trailers, even when the original paving job was done well. The pavement simply was never built for what the facility puts on it. We build heavy-duty concrete truck lanes, loading zones, and trailer parking for warehouses and distribution facilities across the Kansas City metro.

Truck pavement is a different design problem than a car lot. The concrete in a lane that carries semis is thicker than what goes under passenger cars, poured over a compacted base, reinforced so the slab spans weak spots instead of breaking over them, and jointed with dowels that carry wheel loads from one panel to the next. Dock approaches and dolly pads take the most abuse, since trailer landing gear and drive axles sit motionless on one patch of slab for hours at a stretch. We size each area for the loads it will actually see, so you're not paying to pave the whole site to the heaviest spec.

We handle both new construction and remove-and-replace, and most replacement calls come from facilities that can't shut down. There's no charge to have us walk the site, and the written proposal maps which sections have failed and which are getting close. Warehouses in the Lenexa, Olathe, and KCK corridors run around the clock, and a paving schedule that ignores that is useless, so sequencing gets worked out with your operations people before anything is booked. Failed sections come out and go back a few dock positions or one stretch of lane at a time, with a truck route kept open, and the facility keeps shipping while the pavement gets replaced under it.

Common questions

Heavy-Duty Concrete Truck Lanes & Warehouse Paving FAQs

We ship 24/7. How does concrete replacement work around that?

The working rule is that we never demolish more concrete than we can pour and cure on the agreed schedule, so an open truck route is never hostage to a weather delay. With a standard mix, a new section typically needs about seven days before it carries a loaded trailer again, and demolition is scheduled around that reality instead of hoping. The slab keeps gaining strength until full cure at 30 days, but it is ready for use well before that. When a facility has to be running again in two or three days, a faster-curing mix gets it there. That mix costs more, so we price it as an option where the schedule calls for it.

How much thicker does truck-lane concrete need to be than a regular parking lot?

Most of our heavy-duty concrete runs 8 to 10 inches thick, and where a given lane lands in that range depends on the loads. A lane carrying loaded semis all day needs that thickness plus reinforcement and dowelled joints that a light-duty lot usually skips. What sets the spec is your traffic. Axle weights, trucks per day, and whether they're turning or braking on that section. Base, drainage, and slope get the same attention on every job, because a thick slab over a wet base still fails. We state the designed thickness and reinforcement in writing, so a lower bid has to show what it left out.

Our asphalt dock approaches rut every summer. Is concrete the fix?

Usually, yes. Dock approaches take slow, heavy traffic in the same small area, which is the exact condition asphalt handles worst and concrete handles well. We saw-cut the failed asphalt, rebuild and compact the base underneath, and pour reinforced concrete aprons tied into the surrounding pavement. The asphalt parking fields can stay, since they're fine for what they carry. Plan on each apron being out of service through demolition, the pour, and about a week of cure with a standard mix, which is why we replace them a few at a time rather than all at once.

How long will concrete truck lanes last under daily semi traffic?

Designed for the loads and poured over a sound base, decades. Twenty to thirty years is a fair expectation for a heavy-duty lane, where asphalt in the same service can rut badly within a few years. The routine upkeep is joint sealing, so water never reaches the base. What shortens concrete's life is underdesign. A slab specced for cars will crack and fault under semis no matter how well it was poured, so we ask about your truck counts before we talk numbers.
