Concrete Sidewalks in the Kansas City metro

A raised sidewalk edge is a trip hazard, and for an HOA or a property manager it's a claim waiting to happen. A vertical offset of a quarter inch is enough to catch a toe, and when someone falls, the claim usually lands on whoever owns or maintains the walk. Most of our sidewalk work in the KC metro exists because of that exposure. We survey the walks, chalk every panel that has heaved or cracked past a safe offset, and replace only what has failed, so the hazards come off the property without paying to redo concrete that's still doing its job.

Sidewalk panels here mostly fail for one of two reasons. Tree roots lift them from below, and water moves the clay subgrade under them. A parkway tree planted a few feet off the walk decades ago has roots under it now, and a low corner that stays wet settles a little more every year. Replacing a lifted panel without dealing with the root or the drainage means replacing it again in a few years, so we look at the cause before we quote. When the offset is small and the panel is sound, grinding the edge is often the cheaper fix, and we put it on the table unprompted.

ADA compliance drives a lot of this work. Curb ramps need a compliant running slope, a cross slope under two percent, and detectable warning domes where the ramp meets the street. The same two percent limit applies to the walk itself, so a tilted panel can be out of compliance before anyone trips on it. We build ramps and accessible routes for commercial sites and HOAs, and we handle the right-of-way permits and inspections on public walks. Our curb and sidewalk work in Warrensburg, Oak Grove, and Leavenworth is public work of exactly that kind. For a board, the paperwork is half the job. Every flagged panel goes in the proposal with its location and the reason it needs work, so nobody approves a scope sight unseen.

Common questions

Concrete Sidewalks FAQs

Should we replace individual panels or the whole run?

Per-panel replacement is right when most of the walk is sound and the failures are scattered. You pay only for the panels that have moved, and the new concrete ties into the old at existing joints. A full run makes more sense when a root system or a drainage problem runs the length of the walk, or when so many panels have failed that saving the survivors costs more than finishing the job. The survey usually settles it, because the panel count makes the math plain.

What triggers ADA requirements on sidewalk work?

New construction and alterations trigger them. Replace a panel along an accessible route and the new panel needs a compliant cross slope even if the old one never had it. Rebuild a ramp and it needs current slopes and detectable warnings. Maintenance that leaves the surface in place, like grinding an edge, generally doesn't trigger an upgrade. Work in the public right-of-way follows the city's own standards. We sort out which requirements apply during the site walk so nothing shows up later as a change order.

How long before residents can walk on new panels?

Two days behind barricades is the norm; cold weather adds a day or two. Concrete spends about a month climbing to its design number, but a person walking across a panel needs only a fraction of it. On occupied properties we replace panels in a sequence that never cuts off a building entrance, barricade the fresh pours, and open sections as they cure. Residents lose a short stretch of walk for a day or two, not the whole network at once.

Do we need a city permit for sidewalk replacement?

Street-side walks almost always sit in public right-of-way, and most metro cities want paperwork before you cut into one, sometimes an inspection on both sides of the pour. Each city also writes its own spec for thickness, joints, and ramps. Chasing all of that down is our errand, not yours, and it happens before demolition gets scheduled. Walks inside a private community are usually exempt, though we check instead of assuming.
